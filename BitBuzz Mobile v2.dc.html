The thirty-second version
{{ k.text }}

The launch window opens in a nine-day band next March, and for the first time the schedule was published before the hardware was finished. That order matters. Teams building to a public date behave differently to teams building to an internal one.

Three subsystems are on the critical path. The descent radar is the one engineers keep naming when you ask them what keeps them up, because it is the piece with the least flight heritage and the shortest test queue.

"We are not short of ideas. We are short of test slots."
Systems lead, speaking on background

What the schedule actually buys

A fixed window converts an open-ended engineering problem into a queue. Suppliers get a date they can plan shifts around, and the review board gets a reason to say no to late changes.

Integration floor, clean room two. The descent radar has the shortest test queue of the three critical subsystems.

For students watching this, the interesting part is not the rocket. It is that the whole programme is now legible from outside — dates, reviews and slip risk are all public, which makes it something you can actually learn from.
